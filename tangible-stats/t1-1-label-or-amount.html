Label or amount?

The first question in statistics. You ask it about every column of data, before any chart or formula. Make a guess first: you will see it again at the end.

3Four levels of measurement

Four levels, four powers

Climb the stairs. Each step unlocks one more thing you are allowed to say about two values.

Nominal and ordinal are Tag’s levels. Interval and ratio are Ruler’s.
In French: échelle nominale, ordinale, d’intervalle, de rapport
3Four levels of measurement

Is 20° twice as warm as 10°?

Today it is 20°, yesterday it was 10°. Switch the unit and watch “twice” fall apart.

No real zero, no “twice”. Degrees Celsius are an interval scale. Kelvin starts at “no heat at all”, so it is a ratio scale.

4Photo or film

Photo or film?

Five stands sell at the Saturday market, week after week. Look across all stands on one day, or follow one stand over time.

Photo: many things, one moment. That is cross-sectional data. Film: one thing, many moments. That is a time series.
In French: données transversales, série chronologique

Checking everyone is a census. Checking a few is a sample. Stirring first means picking the sample at random, so the spoon looks like the pot.

5Population and sample

Two numbers, two names

Each question has a number for the whole population and a number from the sample. They get different names.

Population → Parameter
Sample → Statistic
The parameter stays fixed, but nobody knows it. The statistic is known, but it changes with every new sample.
In French: paramètre, statistique

5Population and sample

Short names for the numbers

Statisticians write these numbers as symbols. Tap a row to see Lina’s version. No calculating today, just names.

Population: μ, σ, p. Sample: x̄, s, p̄. The bar on x̄ and p̄ means “from the sample”.
5Population and sample

One jar, many spoons

Back to Lina’s taro question. All Québec fans are a jar. Each time Lina asks 20 new fans, she takes a new spoon from the same jar.

The jar’s number (the parameter) never moves. The spoon’s number (the statistic) jumps from scoop to scoop.
Instrument: Jar and spoons. Back in T1.5, T3.3 and Themes 5 to 8.
In French: fluctuation d’échantillonnage
All fans: the population
20 asked: the sample
5Population and sample

See it, or guess it?

Describing tells what is in your hands. Inferring makes a careful guess about the whole jar, and says how sure it is.

Themes 1 to 4 teach you to see. Themes 5 to 8 teach you to guess well.
In French: statistique descriptive, statistique inférentielle

See it

Describe the data in your hands. “6 of my 20 fans chose taro.”

Guess it

Infer something about the whole jar. “About 30% of all fans, give or take.”

6The trap

Digits in disguise

Lina writes 1 for “drink here” and 2 for “to go”. Her spreadsheet happily averages them.

Before you average, ask: is this number an amount, or a name tag?

Statisticians have argued about this for a long time. A famous 1953 article by Frederic Lord, about the numbers on football jerseys, started a debate that is still going.

Sources and textbook links

The definitions on this page follow chapter 1 of the course textbook: elements, variables and observations; categorical and quantitative data; the four scales of measurement; cross-sectional and time series data; population, sample, descriptive and inferential statistics. The discrete/continuous distinction follows OpenIntro Statistics, chapter 1.
