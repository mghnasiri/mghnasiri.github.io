1Spread

First try: longest minus shortest

Lina’s first five customers waited 1, 3, 4, 5 and 7 minutes (invented). The bracket runs from the shortest wait to the longest. Drag the dots.

Range = largest value − smallest value. Quick, but only the two end dots decide it.
In French: étendue
2The squares

How far from the pivot?

A better idea: use every dot. Each wait gets a bar to the balance point: minus to the left, plus to the right. Drag a dot and add them up.

Distances to the mean always add up to zero: that is why the plank balances. Their plain average can’t measure spread.
In French: écart à la moyenne
2The squares

Each bar grows a square

The trick that stops the cancelling: turn each bar into a square, with the bar as its side. An area is never negative. Drag a dot away and watch its square.

Squaring removes the minus signs, and far dots count extra: twice as far makes a square four times as big.
Instrument: Squares. Back in T3.2, T8.1 and T8.2.
2The squares

One average square

These five waits are everyone Lina served in her first hour: the whole crowd. Three steps pour five squares into one.

Variance = the area of the average square. Standard deviation = its side: back in minutes, like the waits.
In French: variance · écart type

2The squares

One number for the spread

Back to the two stands: eight waits each, taken as the whole crowd. The yellow band reaches one standard deviation to each side of the mean.

The standard deviation says roughly how far a typical value sits from the mean, in the data’s own unit.

3Why n − 1

Four free, one forced

Five friends chip in $20 for a gift: $4 each on average. Drag what four of them pay. The friend with the lock pays whatever is left.

Once the mean is known, only n − 1 of the n values are free: the last one is forced. n − 1 is called the degrees of freedom.
In French: degrés de liberté · back in T6.2 and T8.3

3Why n − 1

Draw the bullseye afterwards

Five darts on a wall. The pink diamond is the real bullseye. The green triangle is the middle of your own five darts. The ring shows how far the darts typically land from the spot you measure from.

A small group sits tightest around its own middle. Around the true target it can only look looser.

3Why n − 1

A spoonful does the same

A spoonful of waits can only measure from its own mean. This big jar holds waits of 1 to 7 minutes in equal numbers, so its true mean is 4. Slide the yellow marker: where is the total area smallest?

Measured from its own mean, a sample’s squares are as small as they can be. So a spoonful looks calmer than the jar really is.
Instrument: Jar and spoons, from T1.1.
3Why n − 1

Share among n, or n − 1?

The jar’s true variance is 4. Each spoonful shares its squares two ways: among all n waits, or among n − 1. Scoop many times and compare the long-run averages with 4.

Sharing among n runs too small, by the factor (n − 1)/n. Sharing among n − 1 hits the true variance on average.

!The trap

Variance 4. Four what?

Lina’s five waits: variance 4, standard deviation 2. A customer asks one question. Answer before you look.

The variance is an area, in squared units. Say the standard deviation out loud: it speaks the data’s own unit.

4Compare

Big compared with what?

Two of Lina’s machines (invented). The cup filler pours 500 ml on average, with a standard deviation of 10 ml. The syrup pump gives 30 ml, with a standard deviation of 3 ml.

Coefficient of variation = standard deviation ÷ mean × 100%. It sets a spread next to its own mean, and it has no unit.
In French: coefficient de variation

Sources and textbook links

The range, variance, standard deviation and coefficient of variation, and the notation s², s, σ², σ, n and N, follow chapter 3 of the course textbook [1]. The 244 restaurant tables are real data [2]. Deviation, variance and standard deviation are defined as in [3]. Two standard results stand behind the n − 1 scenes [4]: the squares are smallest when they are measured from the sample’s own mean, and dividing by n − 1 makes the sample variance right on average. The idea of degrees of freedom follows [5]: each relation the values must satisfy removes one. The two stands, Lina’s waits, the jar of waits, the two machines and the three doors are invented for teaching. “In French” labels give common French statistical vocabulary, not quotations from the book.
