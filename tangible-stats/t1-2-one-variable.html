Count the cups

On Saturday Lina sold 40 cups. Her list is a mess. Sort it, then count.

A frequency table lists each label and how many times it appears. That count is its frequency.

1Counting labels

Out of how many?

14 mango cups: is that a lot? It depends on the total. Each square below is one of the 40 cups.

Relative frequency = frequency ÷ total. Percent frequency = relative frequency × 100. They always add up to 1, or 100%.
In French: fréquence relative, fréquence en pourcentage
2Pictures for labels

One bar per label

A bar chart gives each label its own bar. The gaps between bars say: these are separate boxes.

Bar chart: one bar per category, with gaps. For ordered labels, keep their natural order.
In French: diagramme en barres

Cup size: ordered labels keep their order

2Pictures for labels

A pie shows parts of one whole

Each slice is a share of Sunday’s 40 cups, and the slices add up to 100%. Now try to compare two slices.

Pie charts show shares of one whole. To compare close values, bars are easier: our eyes judge lengths better than angles.
In French: diagramme circulaire

Using the pie only: which slice is bigger?

Build classes in three steps

The textbook’s recipe: choose how many classes, find their width, set their limits.

Width ≈ (largest − smallest) ÷ number of classes, rounded up. A common guideline: 5 to 20 classes.

3Amounts need classes

Count each class, then draw

Five classes of $10. Tap a row of the table and its bar rises.

A histogram is a bar chart of the classes. Its bars touch, because the number line has no gaps.
In French: histogramme
!The trap

Gaps or no gaps?

A bar chart and a histogram look alike. Their gaps tell them apart. Try breaking each rule.

Gaps: separate labels (bar chart). Touching: one number line cut into classes (histogram).

4Bin width

Same data, new picture

Change the number of classes. The bars change. The 244 dots underneath never move.

A new bin width changes the picture, not the data.

5Reading shape

Where is the tail?

Slide to change the shape. The name comes from the tail, not from the hump.

Skewed right: a long tail to the right. Skewed left: a long tail to the left. Symmetric: both sides match.
In French: asymétrique à droite, asymétrique à gauche, symétrique
tail on the left ◂ slide ▸ tail on the right
5Reading shape

How many humps?

Lina’s orders through the day: a lunch rush, and maybe an after-school rush. Slide the second rush away from lunch.

One hump: unimodal. Two humps: bimodal. Two humps often mean two different groups mixed together.
In French: unimodale, bimodale

Read the real bills

The waiter’s 244 bills again, this time in classes of $5. Read the picture and answer.

Read a histogram in three moves: the tallest bar, the tail, and how much lies below a value.
6Running totals

Running totals

How many tables paid less than $30? Add the classes as you go, like stacking blocks.

Cumulative frequency = how many values are below the end of each class. The last one is always the total.
In French: fréquences cumulées
6Running totals

The ogive: read “less than”

Join the running totals, as percents, with a line. Drag to any bill and read how many tables paid less.

An ogive climbs from 0% to 100%. Pick a value on the bottom, go up to the line, read the percent below it.
In French: ogive, courbe des fréquences cumulées
7Stem-and-leaf

A histogram that keeps every value

The 19 Friday bills. Split each one: the tens digit is the stem, the ones digit is the leaf. Cents are dropped.

A stem-and-leaf display shows the shape like a histogram, and you can still read every value.
In French: diagramme tige et feuilles
